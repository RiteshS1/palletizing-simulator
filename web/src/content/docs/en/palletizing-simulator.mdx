# What is a palletizing simulator?

**Palletizing** is stacking cartons onto a pallet so they can be wrapped and shipped as one unit. A
**palletizing simulator** lets you plan and inspect those stacks in software before (or instead of)
running a real robot.

## Single-SKU vs mixed-case

- **Single-SKU** palletizing stacks identical boxes in a repeating pattern. The pattern can be
  computed once and reused all day.
- **Mixed-case** palletizing gets cartons of different sizes and weights, often in an order the
  planner does not control, because they arrive on a conveyor. Every pallet needs fresh planning. This
  is the hard version, and the one PalletLab solves.

## The robot cell

A typical cell has an **infeed conveyor**, a **vision system** that measures each carton, an
**industrial arm** with a gripper (usually vacuum suction), and one or more **pallet stations**
marked out with floor tape and guarded by safety fences or light curtains.

## What the planner outputs

For every carton, in order:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `x, y, z` | Where the carton's back-left-bottom corner goes, in mm, in the **pallet frame** |
| `sx, sy, sz` | Its size after any rotation |
| `rotated` | Turned 90° about the vertical axis (cartons stay upright) |
| `placePose` | Where the **tool** must be to release it, in metres, in the **robot base frame**, as a position plus a quaternion |
| `approachPose` | 300 mm straight above `placePose`; the robot descends vertically from here |

A motion planner (for example MoveIt) turns those poses into collision-free joint trajectories.

## The rules every placement must pass

- Inside the pallet footprint and under 1800 mm.
- No overlap with other cartons (touching faces is fine).
- Reachable from above: nothing already sits over the spot.
- At least 75% of the base supported, with the center inside the support polygon.
- Never heavier than the carton below it.
- Total load under 1000 kg.

## What this simulator does not model

Real arm kinematics and collisions with the arm itself, physics (we use rule-based static
stability), sensor noise, and deformable cartons. The **Real-world complexity** chapter explains how
production systems deal with each.
