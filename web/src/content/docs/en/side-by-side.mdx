# Side by side

Claims like "extreme points is better" need evidence. The `bench` app produces it.

## Fair comparison

- **Same inputs:** for every seed both packers get the identical carton set.
- **Many seeds:** 50 per size, because one lucky seed proves nothing.
- **Mean ± standard deviation:** the spread matters as much as the average.

| 40 cartons, 50 seeds | Greedy layer | Extreme points |
|---|---|---|
| Cartons placed | 55.0% ± 8.5 | **90.5% ± 5.7** |
| Pallet filled | 37.4% ± 6.1 | **64.2% ± 4.2** |
| Density | 43.0% ± 7.1 | **65.9% ± 4.7** |
| Center of mass off-centre | 193 ± 37 mm | **73 ± 24 mm** |
| Planning time | **0.05 ms** | 3.2 ms |

Extreme points places more volume on 50 of 50 seeds, and its smaller standard deviation means it is
also more consistent.

## Welford's algorithm

The benchmark computes mean and variance in **one pass** without storing samples:

```cpp
void add(double x) {
    ++n;
    double delta = x - mean;
    mean += delta / n;
    m2 += delta * (x - mean);   // variance = m2 / (n - 1)
}
```

The textbook "average of squares minus square of average" loses precision badly in floating point;
Welford's update is numerically stable.

## Timing

`std::chrono::steady_clock` never jumps (the wall clock can, when it syncs), so it is the right clock
for measuring durations. For micro-benchmarks, Google Benchmark adds warm-up, repetitions and Big-O fitting.
