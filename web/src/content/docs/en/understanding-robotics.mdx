# Understanding robotics

A robot cell is a small factory: sensors tell the software what is there, the software decides what
to do, and actuators do it. This loop, **sense → plan → act**, runs for every carton.

## The parts of a palletizing cell

- **Conveyor** with photo-eyes that detect a carton arriving.
- **Vision:** a 3D camera measures the carton's size and pose; a scanner reads its barcode.
- **Robot arm:** usually 4 axes for palletizing (base, shoulder, elbow, wrist yaw), because cartons
  stay upright, so tilting the tool is never needed. General-purpose arms have 6 axes.
- **End-effector:** a vacuum gripper (suction cups and a vacuum generator) for most cartons;
  fork or clamp grippers for heavy or porous ones.
- **Controller:** the robot vendor's real-time motion controller, plus a PLC coordinating conveyors,
  stations and safety.

## Frames and transforms

Every position needs a reference: a **frame** is an origin plus three axes. A cell has many: world,
robot base, tool, camera, pallet. Converting between them is a **rigid transform**: a rotation plus a
translation, written as a 4 × 4 matrix

```text
T_base_tool = T_base_pallet · T_pallet_tool
```

Read it right to left: a pose known in the pallet frame, chained through where the pallet sits
relative to the robot, gives the pose the robot needs. PalletLab's `robot_cell.cpp` does exactly
this with Eigen's `Isometry3d`. The live model on this page shows the `world`, `robot_base`,
`pallet` and `tool` frames; watch the tool frame jump to each carton's place pose.

## Kinematics

- **Forward kinematics (FK):** joint angles → where the tool is. Easy: multiply one transform per joint.
- **Inverse kinematics (IK):** desired tool pose → joint angles. Harder: there may be several
  solutions (elbow up or down), or none if the pose is out of reach. 4-axis palletizers have simple
  closed-form IK; 6-axis arms use analytic solvers or numerical optimisation.

## Rotations and quaternions

Rotations can be stored as matrices, Euler angles or **quaternions** (`x, y, z, w`). Quaternions avoid
gimbal lock and interpolate smoothly, so ROS and most robot APIs use them. A rotation is always a
**unit** quaternion, which is something our tests check.

## The pick-and-place cycle

1. Detect and measure the carton on the conveyor.
2. Plan its spot on the pallet (this project's job).
3. Move above the pick point, descend, switch vacuum on, verify the grip (vacuum pressure sensor).
4. Move to the **approach pose** above the place spot, descend vertically to the **place pose**.
5. Release, lift away, repeat. A good cell does this in 6–10 seconds.

**"Which frame is that pose in?"** is the most common source of robotics bugs. Name the frame in
variable names (`T_base_pallet`) and keep units consistent (ROS uses metres and radians).
</Interview>
