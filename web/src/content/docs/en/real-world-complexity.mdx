# Real-world complexity

The planner in this project is clean: exact sizes, known weights, perfect placement. Real cells are
not. Here is what production systems handle, and how.

## Perception is noisy

Cameras measure sizes and poses with millimetre-level error, worse with shiny tape, dark cartons or
sagging lids. Planners add **clearance margins** between cartons and verify each placement with a
second measurement. Our integer-millimetre geometry assumes exact numbers; a real system would add
tolerances to every comparison.

## Cartons are not perfect boxes

Cartons arrive dented, bulging or with weak corners. A **crush-strength** model (how much load a
carton can carry, based on its board grade and size) replaces our simple "never heavier on lighter"
rule, and the load is summed down the whole support graph, not just pairwise.

## Weights may be unknown

Without a check-weigher, weight comes from master data that may be wrong. Robots measure the load at
the tool (payload estimation) and planners stay conservative.

## The order of arrival

Cartons arrive in an order the planner does not choose. Real systems use a **buffer** (a few cartons
of lookahead on a conveyor or a staging area) to reorder them, which is the online-vs-offline
packing gap you can see in the **Orders** chapter.

## Cycle time

A cell might need a carton placed every 6–10 seconds. Planning must fit well inside that budget,
which is why heuristics running in milliseconds beat exact solvers that may need minutes.

## Robot reachability and collisions

The arm itself must not hit the stack, the fence or itself. Our "reachable from above" rule is a
simplified version; a real planner checks the gripper's footprint and approach path against the
whole planning scene.

## Safety

Industrial robots follow safety standards such as **ISO 10218** (robot and cell safety) and, for
collaborative robots, **ISO/TS 15066**. Fences, light curtains and safety-rated controllers stop
motion when a person enters the cell. Software never replaces these hardware safety layers.

## Stability in transport

A pallet that stands still may still shift in a truck. Planners keep the **center of mass low and
centred**, interlock layers where possible, and the pallet is stretch-wrapped before it ships.
