# Depalletizing

At the other end of the supply chain, pallets arrive at a store or distribution centre and have to
be taken apart. **Depalletizing** is palletizing in reverse, and in some ways harder.

## Why it is harder

- **Unknown layout.** The robot didn't build this pallet. It has to discover where each carton is,
  every time, with 3D vision (see **Perception and vision**).
- **Unknown sizes.** Mixed pallets from suppliers have no reliable layout data; sizes come from what
  the camera sees, and only the tops are visible.
- **Shifted loads.** Transport makes cartons lean, slip and dent; nothing sits where it was planned.

## The order matters: top first

A carton can only be picked if nothing rests on it and the gripper can reach its top face. That is
the **reachability rule** again, in reverse: pick from the highest layer down. Within a layer, a good
order also keeps the remaining stack stable (don't leave a tall, thin tower standing alone).

The live model runs the planner's own plan **backwards**: the last carton placed is the first one
removed, rising with the gripper.

## The pick loop

1. Capture a point cloud of the whole pallet.
2. Detect the top-most cartons and their poses.
3. Choose a carton that is reachable, fully visible and safe to remove.
4. Pick it, verify the grip (vacuum sensor), place it on the outfeed conveyor.
5. Repeat, re-scanning as the stack changes.

## Graphs and ordering

"Which cartons support which" is a directed graph: an edge from A to B means A rests on B. A valid
unstacking order is a **topological order** of that graph (remove a node only once nothing points
into it), which is exactly what top-first achieves. The same structure, reversed, is a valid stacking order.

## Measuring the cell

Depalletizing cells are judged on **picks per hour**, **pick success rate** and how often a person
has to intervene. Good perception and a cautious choice of the next carton matter more than raw arm speed.
